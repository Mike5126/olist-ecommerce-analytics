# Olist E-Commerce Analytics — Business Insights Report

**Project:** Olist E-Commerce Analytics Portfolio  
**Data source:** Brazilian Olist public e-commerce dataset, modeled in PostgreSQL  
**Reporting period:** 2016–2018; monthly trend analysis focuses on January 2017–August 2018  
**Currency:** Brazilian real (BRL)  
**Prepared for:** Data Analyst Portfolio

## 1. Executive Summary

The analysis examines delivered-product revenue, purchase frequency, regional delivery performance, and seller revenue concentration. The PostgreSQL `mart` layer supports four Power BI dashboard pages: Executive Overview, Customer Analytics, Delivery Performance, and Seller Performance. Overall dashboard KPIs were cross-checked against SQL using all-period filters.

Key findings:

1. **Revenue growth in November 2017 was volume-led.** Delivered-product revenue reached **BRL 987,765.37**, a **52.37% month-over-month increase**. Delivered orders increased **62.77%**, while average order value (AOV) declined from **BRL 144.76** to **BRL 135.51** (approximately **−6.39%**).
2. **Repeat purchasing was uncommon within the observed window.** **90,557 of 93,358** customers (**97.00%**) had only one delivered order; **2,801 (3.00%)** had two or more. Among **3,120** consecutive repeat-purchase intervals, mean time between orders was **79.15 days** and median was **29 days**.
3. **Next-month cohort retention was consistently below 1%.** Across the 14 cohorts with six months of observable follow-up (January 2017–February 2018), M1 retention ranged from **0.18%** (February 2017) to **0.72%** (October 2017).
4. **Late-delivery exposure varied by state.** Among states with at least 100 valid deliveries, **Alagoas (AL)** had the highest late-delivery rate (**21.41%**). **Rio de Janeiro (RJ)** combined a **12.11%** late rate with **1,495** late orders, while **São Paulo (SP)** had the most late orders (**1,820**) but a lower late rate (**4.49%**).
5. **Seller revenue was concentrated.** The top **310 of 3,095** sellers (top 10%, rounded up) generated **BRL 8,994,978.42**, or **68.03%**, of total delivered-product sales.

These are descriptive findings, not proof of causal mechanisms. Recommendations below are hypotheses for validation and experimentation.

## 2. Business Objectives and Analytical Scope

The analysis addresses five questions:

- What drove monthly changes in delivered-product sales: order count, AOV, or both?
- Which product categories contributed to the November 2017 sales increase?
- How frequently do observed customers place repeat delivered orders, and how does retention differ by acquisition cohort?
- Which customer states face the largest delivery-delay risks by rate and by absolute number of orders?
- How concentrated is sales revenue among marketplace sellers?

### Definitions

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Delivered Product Sales | Sum of `mart.fact_order_items.delivered_item_sales`, excluding freight by this project definition. |
| Delivered Orders (revenue analysis) | Distinct delivered `order_id` counted from `mart.fact_order_items` for a purchase month. |
| AOV | Delivered Product Sales / distinct delivered orders in the relevant period. |
| Active Customer | Distinct `customer_unique_id` with at least one delivered order in `mart.customer_order_activity`. |
| Repeat Customer | Active customer with at least two distinct delivered orders during the observation period. |
| Monthly Cohort Retention (Mx) | Share of first-purchase cohort with at least one delivered order in calendar month x after cohort month. This is *monthly*, not cumulative, retention. |
| Valid Delivery | Delivered order with non-null delivered timestamp, non-null delivery days, and non-negative delivery days. |
| Late Delivery | Valid delivery with `is_late = 1`; rate is late deliveries / valid deliveries. |
| Active Seller | Distinct seller with at least one delivered order. |
| Top 10% Seller Share | Sales generated by the highest-ranked 310 of 3,095 sellers / total delivered-product sales, including zero-sales sellers in the ranking universe. |

## 4. Revenue and Growth Analysis

### 4.1 Monthly performance and growth drivers

The strongest observed monthly delivered-product sales figure was **November 2017**, with **BRL 987,765.37** across **7,289** delivered orders. Compared with October 2017:

| Metric | Oct 2017 | Nov 2017 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Delivered orders | 4,478 | 7,289 | +62.77% |
| Delivered-product sales | BRL 648,247.65 | BRL 987,765.37 | +52.37% |
| AOV | BRL 144.76 | BRL 135.51 | approx. −6.39% |

**Finding R1 — November growth was driven by order volume rather than higher order value.** Since sales = delivered orders × AOV, the increase in orders more than compensated for the reduction in AOV.

**Implication:** A sales spike is not necessarily evidence of customers purchasing more expensive baskets. **Recommendation:** Analyze item counts, category mix, selling prices, discounts (if available), and campaign calendars (if available) before attributing growth to promotions or seasonality.

Selected 2018 month-over-month results further show the interplay of order count and AOV:

| Month | Orders MoM | Sales MoM | AOV (BRL) | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Mar 2018 | +6.83% | +15.36% | 136.14 | Sales grew faster than orders; AOV increased. |
| Apr 2018 | −2.93% | +2.12% | 143.21 | AOV offset a decline in orders. |
| Jun 2018 | −9.63% | −12.43% | 140.36 | Both order volume and AOV declined. |
| Aug 2018 | +3.12% | −3.38% | 132.04 | AOV decline outweighed order growth. |

**Methodological note:** Raw `lag()` over only months containing transactions may compare non-consecutive calendar months. The corrected monthly comparison joins each month to the immediately preceding calendar month. Highly sparse 2016 months are excluded from substantive month-over-month interpretation; the 2017–August 2018 analysis still has a limited observation window.

### 4.2 Categories contributing to November 2017 growth

Watches & Gifts generated the largest *November revenue* among categories in the top-10 query (**BRL 95,292.34**), but **Bed, Bath & Table** generated the largest *absolute increase* from October to November (**BRL 41,949.93**).

| Category | Oct sales (BRL) | Nov sales (BRL) | Absolute increase (BRL) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Bed, Bath & Table | 46,007.70 | 87,957.63 | **41,949.93** |
| Health & Beauty | 40,698.99 | 78,274.40 | 37,575.41 |
| Home Decor | 30,009.74 | 62,091.27 | 32,081.53 |
| Watches & Gifts | 64,874.63 | 95,292.34 | 30,417.71 |
| Toys | 33,324.42 | 62,611.26 | 29,286.84 |

**Finding R2 — Top sales and top contributions to growth are different rankings.** Bed, Bath & Table alone accounted for roughly **12.36%** of the overall **BRL 339,517.72** month-over-month increase.

**Implication and recommendation:** For merchandising decisions, examine both absolute sales and incremental sales contribution. Follow up with order-volume, item-count, price, and seller-level breakdowns for categories that contributed to the increase.

## 5. Customer Retention and Purchase Behavior

### 5.1 Purchase-frequency distribution

| Distinct delivered orders per customer | Customers | Share of active customers |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 90,557 | 97.00% |
| 2 | 2,573 | 2.76% |
| 3 | 181 | 0.19% |
| 4 | 28 | 0.03% |
| 5 or more | 19 | ~0.02% |

**Finding C1 — Only 3.00% of observed customers had multiple delivered orders.** The figure describes this dataset and its time window; it does not distinguish one-time demand from customer churn or capture future orders outside the dataset.

### 5.2 Time between consecutive orders

Across **3,120** observed consecutive purchase intervals, mean time between delivered orders was **79.15 days**, versus a median of **29.00 days**.

**Finding C2 — Repeat-purchase intervals are right-skewed.** Some long intervals raise the mean above the median. This metric uses intervals, not an equal-weight average of customer-specific means; orders on the same date may contribute zero-day intervals.

**Recommendation:** Test repurchase communications segmented by product category and purchase behavior; a 30-day reminder is a candidate experiment, not a universally optimal interval.

**Recommendation:** Segment cohorts by first-purchase category, geographic market, and order value, and measure subsequent retention under equal follow-up windows. Validate any proposed campaign via a controlled experiment.

## 6. Delivery Performance and Regional Risk

Across **96,470** valid delivered orders, **6,534** were late (**6.77%**), while **89,936** were on time (**93.23%**). Mean delivery duration was **12.50 days**; median was **10.00 days**.

### 6.1 States with high late-delivery rates

The ranking below includes only states with at least **100 valid deliveries**.

| Customer state | Valid deliveries | Late orders | Average delivery days | Late rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| AL | 397 | 85 | 24.50 | **21.41%** |
| MA | 717 | 125 | 21.51 | 17.43% |
| SE | 335 | 51 | 21.46 | 15.22% |
| PI | 476 | 66 | 19.39 | 13.87% |
| CE | 1,279 | 176 | 21.20 | 13.76% |
| RJ | 12,350 | 1,495 | 15.24 | 12.11% |
| SP | 40,494 | 1,820 | 8.70 | 4.49% |

**Finding D1 — Risk differs substantially by region.** Alagoas had the highest observed late rate among states passing the 100-delivery threshold, at **21.41%**, approximately **3.16 times** the national rate of **6.77%**.

**Finding D2 — Absolute volume and risk rate indicate different priorities.** São Paulo had **1,820** late orders but only a **4.49%** late rate; Rio de Janeiro had **1,495** late orders and a **12.11%** late rate. RJ is a particularly relevant target for follow-up because both rate and impacted order count are material.

**Recommendations:** Investigate route length, origin-destination pairs, fulfillment lead times, carrier performance, and package characteristics. Prioritize high-impact regions by both expected reduction in late orders and late-delivery risk, rather than ranking solely by percentage.

**Caveat:** Geography is assigned from the customer's state, and the analysis is descriptive. It does not isolate location effects from seller mix, distance, carrier, or item mix.

## 7. Seller Performance and Revenue Concentration

There were **3,095 sellers** in the seller dimension; **2,970 (95.96%)** had at least one delivered order. Average delivered-product sales across **all** sellers were **BRL 4,271.89**.

### 7.1 Concentration among top sellers

| Seller group | Sellers | Delivered-product revenue (BRL) | Share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Highest-ranked 10% | 310 | 8,994,978.42 | **68.03%** |
| Remaining sellers | 2,785 | 4,226,519.69 | 31.97% |
| All sellers | 3,095 | 13,221,498.11 | 100.00% |

**Finding S1 — Delivered-product sales were highly concentrated.** The top 310 sellers generated **68.03%** of sales. The concentration calculation includes all 3,095 sellers in the ranking universe, including those with zero delivered-product sales.

Among the **top 10 individual sellers by sales**, **9** were based in **SP** and **1** in **BA**. This does not in itself demonstrate a higher success rate in SP, because SP's overall share of sellers has not been controlled for.

**Recommendations:** Monitor top-seller concentration and dependence over time; investigate whether high-performing sellers share operational practices worth replicating. Evaluate smaller sellers by active tenure, assortment, fulfillment, and conversion rather than assuming low sales reflect poor performance.

## 9. Limitations and Interpretation Guardrails

1. **Historical and bounded data:** This project analyzes a historical Olist dataset, not present-day business conditions. Customers may repurchase after the observation window; later cohorts have shorter follow-up.
2. **Delivered-only sales scope:** The revenue measure reflects delivered-product sales, not all payments, gross merchandise value including freight, accounting revenue, returns, or profit.
3. **Sparse early months:** September and December 2016 each contain one observed delivered order in the monthly revenue result. Extremely large or negative naive MoM figures are not reliable business trend measures. November 2016 is absent from transactional monthly output.
4. **Definition alignment:** AOV is sales divided by distinct delivered orders; seller-level delivered-order counts may differ from platform-wide counts if one order contains items from several sellers. Delivery rate denominators exclude eight records with missing/invalid delivery information.
5. **Same-day intervals:** Repurchase intervals use calendar `purchase_date`, so two orders on one day have a zero-day gap. Mean 79.15 days is interval-weighted, not customer-weighted.
6. **Cohort interpretation:** Cohort cells show activity in each *specific* month, not rolling or cumulative retention. The heatmap was restricted to cohorts with six observable months.
7. **Regional confounding:** Late rates by customer state do not adjust for distance, product mix, carrier, order complexity, or seasonal effects. The 100-order cutoff is an analytical filtering choice.
8. **Seller tenure and mix:** Top-seller revenue concentration does not establish profitability, efficiency, or seller quality. Seller lifecycle effects have not been adjusted for.
9. **Validation boundary:** Core all-period dashboard KPIs were reconciled to SQL; multi-slicer intersections and all chart-level results have not been exhaustively validated.
10. **No causal attribution:** Observational aggregates cannot confirm promotion effects, customer satisfaction drivers, or the operational cause of delivery delay.

## 10. Conclusion

The Olist analysis demonstrates SQL-based data modeling, KPI validation, time-series decomposition, cohort retention analysis, geographic service-performance analysis, and seller concentration measurement. The strongest portfolio findings are the **volume-led November 2017 sales increase**, **low observed repeat-purchase frequency**, **uneven regional late-delivery exposure**, and **68.03% revenue share held by the top 10% of sellers**.

A sensible next stage would be to validate the visual-level filters and then design deeper diagnostics or controlled experiments around customer retention and logistics. The report supplies evidence-based priorities while distinguishing observation from causation.
